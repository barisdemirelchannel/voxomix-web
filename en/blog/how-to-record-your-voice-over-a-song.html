How to Record Your Voice Over a Song

From muting the vocals to measuring latency, playing the take back and exporting the file

Published: November 25, 2026 · ~7 min read

Short answer: to record your voice over a song, first mute the original vocals, then put on headphones so the song doesn't leak back into the microphone. Measure your microphone latency once, then record while the song plays. Play the take back with the song; if you don't like it, delete it and try again. You can do all four steps on one screen without learning an audio editor.

This guide is for anyone who wants to improve their singing or playing by hearing themselves on a real song. If you've tried recording with a phone, you know the problems: the song bleeds into the take, your voice lands late, or the recording doesn't start together with the backing track. Below we explain the cause of each problem and how to fix it, then walk through the same job in VoxoMix. VoxoMix is our app, and we'll be clear about which step needs which plan.

What you need: a computer, a mic, headphones and the song

You don't need an expensive studio to start. Four things are enough:

  • A computer: a Mac with Apple Silicon (M-series) or a Windows 10/11 PC.
  • A microphone: the built-in mic works; a USB mic or a mic plugged into an audio interface sounds cleaner.
  • Headphones: closed-back, over-ear headphones are best. Open-back models leak sound.
  • The song as an audio file: an MP3, WAV or similar. It should be your own recording or a song you have the right to use.

The room matters too. Carpets, curtains and a full bookshelf cut down reflections from the walls. In an empty room with hard surfaces, the take sounds echoey. Recording in front of an open wardrobe full of clothes is an old trick that still works.

Why mute the original vocals?

If the singer is still in the mix, you hear two voices at once. It gets hard to tell where your own pitch drifts. With the vocals muted you hear only the backing track, and your voice is the only voice on the take.

That means splitting the song into channels: vocals, drums, bass and the other instruments. We explain how that works in how to remove vocals from a song. If you only want to sing along without recording, the guide to making a karaoke version of any song is all you need. This article goes one step further: recording yourself and listening back.

A tip for your first tries: instead of muting the vocals completely, leave them quiet. You still hear the melody, but your own voice stays on top. Mute them fully once you feel confident.

Why wear headphones while recording?

If the song plays through speakers while you record, the microphone hears the song too. The take ends up with a second, slightly late copy of the backing track. When you play it back with the song, that copy sounds like an echo or a muddy smear.

Headphones solve this. The song goes only to your ears, and the mic hears only you. Don't turn them up too loud; at high volume even closed headphones leak a little.

Then there's hearing yourself. With headphones on, your own voice sounds quieter, and you can start pushing or drifting off pitch without noticing. That's why good recording apps feed your voice back into the headphones, which is called monitoring. Sliding one ear cup slightly off is the old-school version of the same fix.

Why does the recording land behind the song?

Every recording on a computer has a small delay. The mic signal is converted to digital, the operating system holds it in buffers, and only then does it reach the recording app. The delay is short, but your ear can still hear it as "my voice is a bit late".

This is one reason a take can sound slightly behind the backing track. The fix is to measure the delay once and shift the take earlier by that amount. This is called latency calibration. Measure again whenever you change your audio interface, microphone or computer.

How do you record over a song in VoxoMix?

VoxoMix splits the song into channels on your own computer; the song is never uploaded to a server. Below the separated channels there is a Record channel. Here are the steps:

  1. Add the song and split it. Drag the file into the app; when the split finishes, the channels appear one under another.
  2. Mute the vocals. Press M (mute) on the vocal channel, or turn it down.
  3. Measure latency once. Open the Fix Latency window with the clock icon on the Record channel, choose the microphone you'll use and press Measure Latency. Run this measurement with your speakers and microphone, not headphones. When it finishes, your take is aligned automatically.
  4. Put on your headphones. If you like, turn on Monitor yourself while recording (headphones) to hear your voice together with the song.
  5. Record. Press the ● button on the Record channel and sing over the song. Stop when you're done.

If the song sits too high or too low for your voice, change the key of the song before you record. To practice a hard part slowly first, slow the song down without changing the pitch.

How do you play back and improve your take?

Once you stop, the Record channel behaves like any other channel. Play the song from the start and your voice comes in together with the backing track. You can turn the channel up or down, mute it with M, or listen to it on its own.

Listen for three things:

  • Pitch: note where you drift. Long held notes and big jumps are the usual trouble spots.
  • Timing: are you late or early on your entries? Bring the original vocal back in quietly and compare.
  • Breath: if your voice sags at the end of phrases, move your breaths.

Each song keeps one take. When you record again, the app asks first and then replaces the old take with the new one. Use the trash button to delete a take you don't want. For a tricky passage, loop that section and run it a few times before you record the full take.

The Record channel isn't only for singers. Use the same steps to record your guitar, violin or saxophone over the song: mute your instrument's channel first, then play the part yourself.

How do you get the take as a file?

The download button on the Record channel saves your voice as its own file. The file is the same length as the song and lines up with the song's start. So if you drop it into another music program at the same point as the backing channels, it stays in sync.

The file format depends on your plan: MP3 on Student, with lossless WAV also available on Producer. Producer's batch export downloads every channel at once, and if you've recorded a take, the Record channel is included.

One reminder: your take is yours to listen to. If you want to publish a recording made over someone else's song, you need permission from the rights holder.

Frequently asked questions

What's the easiest way to record my voice over a song?

Open the song in an app that splits it into channels, mute the vocals, put on headphones, measure your mic latency once and record while the song plays. Play the take back with the song and record again if needed.

Why is my recording behind the song?

There's a small delay before the mic signal reaches the computer. A calibration that measures that delay once and shifts the take earlier removes the offset. Measure again when you change your mic or audio interface.

Can I record without headphones?

You can, but the song coming from the speakers also reaches the mic and sounds like an echo on the take. Use closed-back headphones for a clean recording. In VoxoMix, the latency measurement itself is done with speakers and a mic, not headphones.

Is the Record channel included in VoxoMix's free plan?

No. The Record channel is available on the Student and Producer plans. On the free plan you can mute the vocals and sing along, and you can try the Record channel with the Producer trial.

Can I move my take into another music program?

Yes. You download the Record channel as its own file; it's the same length as the song and aligned to its start. Start it at the same point as the backing channels and it stays in sync.

Is my song or my recording uploaded to a server?

No. Separation and recording happen on your Mac (Apple Silicon) or Windows 10/11 computer, and your files aren't uploaded. You need internet for the first sign-in and license check, and the lyrics transcription model is downloaded once the first time you use it.

Conclusion

A clean recording over a song comes down to four steps: mute the vocals, wear headphones, measure latency once, record and listen back. Headphones keep the song out of your take. The latency measurement puts your voice in time with the backing track. Listening to yourself shows exactly which notes and which entries need work.

How to Isolate the Bass From a Song and Learn the Bass Line by Ear

Solo the bass, slow it down, find the roots, then mute it and play along yourself

Published: 2 November 2026 · ~8 min read

Short answer: to learn a song's bass line, open the song in an app that splits it into tracks, listen to the bass on its own, slow down the hard parts and find the notes on your bass. Once the part sits, mute the bass track and play along with the rest of the band. The basic rule of learning by ear stays the same: roots first, then rhythm, then the fills.

The most common problem bass players describe is simple: they can't hear the bass. It hides under the drums and guitars. This guide explains why, gives you an order of work that helps, and shows how to isolate the bass in VoxoMix and learn the part step by step.

Why the bass gets lost in the mix

The lowest string of a four-string bass (E) vibrates at roughly 41 Hz. That is one of the ranges where human hearing is least sensitive. On top of that, the kick drum, the low strings of the guitar, the pianist's left hand and synths all play in the same low register. The result: the bass is there, but it doesn't stand out on its own.

Phone and laptop speakers often reproduce very low notes weakly, so a bass line can even sound like part of the guitar. Three things help: decent headphones, hearing the bass on its own (exactly what stem separation does) and slowing down, because fast passing notes and ghost notes only become audible at a slower tempo.

Start with your ears: the order that works

You can work out a bass line without any tools. Getting the order right is half the job.

1. Hum the bass line

Before you touch the instrument, play a section a few times and follow the bass line with your voice. Finding a melody you can already hum is much easier than finding one that isn't in your head yet.

2. Find the roots

In most pop and rock songs the bass plays the root of the chord when the chord changes. Find the note on the first beat of each chord and write it down. Those notes are the skeleton of the part. We cover the general logic of chords in our guide to finding the chords of a song.

3. Take the rhythm from the drums

The bass very often locks in with the kick drum. Count where the kick lands, and listen to whether the bass holds notes long or short and where it rests. The right notes with the wrong rhythm make a part unrecognisable.

4. Fills last, then pick a position

Passing notes, octave jumps and slides come last. Then choose the position where your hand moves least; an open string sounds brighter than the same note higher up a thicker string, which can be a clue.

This method trains your ear, but it is slow when the bass isn't clear. The steps below follow the same order and add tools that help your ears.

Step by step: isolating the bass in VoxoMix

VoxoMix is a desktop app (Mac and Windows) that splits a song into tracks on your own computer and detects its chords and tempo. The bass comes out as its own track on the free plan too: the free plan's 4-track separation gives you vocals, drums, bass and everything else (Other Instruments).

  1. Open the song. Drag an MP3, WAV, FLAC or M4A file into the app. Separation happens on your computer; the file is never uploaded to a server.
  2. Solo the bass track. Press the S (solo) button next to the bass track. Now only the bass plays, and notes that were buried under drums and guitars become audible.
  3. Slow it down. Use the speed slider to slow the song down to 0.5×. The pitch doesn't change, so the bass notes stay in the same key at the slower speed and you can match them directly on your instrument. More in our guide to slowing a song down without changing the pitch.
  4. Repeat the hard bar. On Basic and above, A–B repeat lets you set A at the start of a section and B at the end; the song plays that stretch over and over. It is the easiest way to hear a fast run ten times in a row.
  5. Check the root with the chord finder. The piano chord finder is available on every plan. The chord name on screen tells you which root the bass is most likely playing at that moment. If the bass plays a different note, it is either a passing note or the chord is an inversion; your ear makes the call.
  6. Hear the bass with the drums. To work out the rhythm, use M (mute) on every track except bass and drums. With just the rhythm section left, you can clearly hear where the kick and the bass hit together.

Write the notes down as you go. Verse and chorus usually repeat, so two sections cover most of the song.

Mute the bass and play along

  • Mute the bass track. Press M on the bass track. The song plays without bass and you fill the gap; with no original bass covering you, every mistake is audible.
  • Start slow, speed up step by step. Play cleanly at around 0.7× first, then raise the speed each pass up to the original tempo. Tempo and the metronome are covered in our guide to finding the BPM of a song; both are on the free plan.
  • Record yourself. Student and Producer include a record channel: with the bass muted, you record your playing over the song while hearing yourself in your headphones, then listen back to hear where your timing drifts.
  • Tune first. The instrument tuner on Student and Producer covers bass guitar too.

If the key is awkward (say, a low part written for a five-string), transpose the song (Basic and above); the bass track moves with it. See changing the key of a song.

Where separation can go wrong

Stem separation brings the bass forward clearly in most pop and rock songs, but it isn't perfect. Check with your ears in these cases:

  • Kick drum bleed. A little of the kick's thump can remain in the bass track. If you hear a hit rather than a note, it is bleed.
  • Synth bass and the piano's left hand. In electronic music the bass is often a synth; separation may put it in the bass track or in Other Instruments. If the bass track sounds empty, listen to the other track too.
  • Heavily distorted bass. Distorted bass tones can blend with guitars. Slow down and listen note by note.
  • Length limit. The maximum song length depends on the plan: 4 minutes on Free, 5 on Basic, 7 on Student, unlimited on Producer.

Listen to each track alone, then all together; you confirm each note on your instrument. For other instruments, see our guide to removing an instrument from a song.

Frequently asked questions

Can I isolate the bass from a song for free?

Yes. On VoxoMix's free plan the song is split into 4 tracks and the bass is its own track; you can process 2 songs a month up to 4 minutes each. You can solo or mute the bass track.

Where should I start when learning a bass line by ear?

With the roots. Each time the chord changes, find the first note the bass plays and write it down; then take the rhythm from the kick drum. Leave passing notes and fills for last.

I can't hear the bass. Is something wrong with my ears?

Most likely not. The bass plays very low frequencies and sits under the drums and guitars, and small speakers reproduce it weakly. Listening on headphones and soloing the bass usually shows the difference right away.

Can I mute the bass and play along with the song?

Yes. The M button on the bass track mutes the bass; the rest of the song keeps playing and you fill the gap. This works on every plan.

Does slowing the bass line down change its pitch?

No. The speed slider slows the song down to 0.5× and the pitch stays the same, so the notes you find are the same notes as at the original tempo.

Does my song leave my computer?

No. VoxoMix splits the song into tracks on your own computer; the file is never uploaded to any server.

Conclusion

Learning a song's bass line is much easier once you can actually hear the bass. Remember the order: solo the bass and slow it down, find the roots first, then the rhythm, mute the bass and play along yourself. When you can play cleanly at a slow tempo, raise the speed step by step to the original tempo.

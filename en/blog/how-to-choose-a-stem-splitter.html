How to Choose a Stem Splitter: 5 Questions to Ask Before You Commit

Online tool, your DAW's built-in splitter, free open-source app or a practice app: pick the category that fits what you actually do

Published: 23 November 2026 · ~9 min read

Short answer: the best stem splitter is the one that fits what you do after the split. Before you pick, answer five questions: can your song be uploaded to a server, which computer will you use, how many stems do you need, what will you do with the stems and how do you want to pay. Then run the same test song through two or three candidates and let your ears decide.

Stem separation is everywhere now: browser tools, built-in splitters added to recent versions of some DAWs, free open-source desktop programs, and apps that combine separation with practice tools. More choice has changed the question. It is no longer "which one can split a song?" but "which one actually helps me?" This guide is not a brand shoot-out. It walks through five categories and the questions that separate them. Our own app, VoxoMix, sits in one of those categories, and we will say so plainly.

The five categories of stem splitters

They all do the same core job: an AI model listens to the mix and splits vocals, drums, bass and other instruments into separate stems. The difference is where the work happens and what you are left with after the split.

CategoryStrengthWatch out for
Online toolsNothing to install; opens on any device, phones included; processing runs on a server.The song is uploaded; queues, file-size caps and minute or credit limits are common.
Your DAW's built-in splitterStems land straight in your project; no extra program if you already work in that DAW.Only in certain versions of certain DAWs; no practice tools; you need to know the DAW.
Free open-source programsFree, run on your computer, wide choice of models.Setup and model choice take time; they produce files only, no practice or chord tools.
Professional audio editorsSeparation plus spectral clean-up and repair tools.Higher price and learning curve; built for production and restoration.
Practice appsCombine separation with slow-down, looping, chords, lyrics and key change on one screen.Song-count and length limits by plan; not meant for detailed audio repair.

There is no "best" column in that table, because there is no single best. An online tool makes sense for someone who wants a quick karaoke track on a phone; a DAW's built-in splitter for someone already mixing in that DAW; an open-source program for someone with zero budget who does not mind setup. The questions below help you find your row.

Question 1: Can your song be uploaded?

With online tools the file is sent to a server for processing. For a song that is already on the radio, that is usually fine. It changes if you work with any of these:

  • Your own unreleased song or demo. Nobody else has the lyrics, melody and chords yet; you may not want them sitting on a server.
  • A client's or a band's recording. You may not have permission to send the file to a third party.
  • An unreliable internet connection. Uploading and downloading large WAV files can take longer than the separation itself.

In those cases, pick a category that processes on your own computer: your DAW's splitter, an open-source program or a practice app that runs locally. We explain why local processing matters for musicians in our offline stem separation guide. Also check what a program means by "offline": some desktop apps need the internet to sign in or verify a licence but never send the audio. The real question is "does my song leave my computer?"

Question 2: Which computer will you use?

With local separation, your processor and graphics chip do the work. So before you download, check three things:

  • Operating system and chip. On Mac, does the program require Apple Silicon (M-series) or does it support older Intel Macs? Which Windows versions does it run on? If the download page does not say, ask.
  • Time. The same song can split in a short time on a powerful machine and take minutes on an older laptop. Measure a full-length song yourself during the trial.
  • Disk space. Separation models and WAV stems take room. Six lossless stems of one song can take several times the space of the original MP3.

With online tools this question turns into a different one: file-size limits and how long you wait in the queue.

Question 3: How many stems do you need?

Programs usually split a song into two, four or six stems:

  • 2 stems: vocals and everything else. Enough for a karaoke backing track or an a cappella. More in our how to remove vocals from a song guide.
  • 4 stems: vocals, drums, bass and other. Often enough for drummers and bass players.
  • 6 stems: adds guitar and piano. If you are a guitarist or pianist who wants to hear your own part separately, look at this row; we show the difference with examples in how to remove an instrument from a song.

The more stems, the more bleed (traces of other instruments) you may hear in each one; that is the nature of separation. So do not pay for more than you need, but if the instrument you play does not come out as its own stem, that program falls short for you.

Question 4: What happens after the split?

This is the question that separates the categories most. Separation is the start; the real work comes after.

If you are practising

A musician learning a part usually needs to mute or solo their instrument, slow the hard section down without changing the pitch, loop the same four bars, see chords and lyrics on screen, and sometimes move the key to suit their voice. With a program that only outputs files, each of these means opening another tool. We cover every step in our complete guide to practising any song, and slowing down in how to slow down a song without changing the pitch.

If you are producing

For remixing, sampling or mixing, look elsewhere: lossless WAV export, exporting all stems at once, stems that line up from the same start point in your DAW and, if needed, MIDI. Your DAW's splitter or a professional editor is a strong candidate here; if you choose a desktop app, check which plan includes those export options.

If you only need a backing track once

If you want an instrumental of a single song for a wedding, a quick free option may be enough. Even then, look at Question 1 one more time.

Question 5: How do you want to pay?

Four pricing models are common in this category:

  • Free: open-source programs and the free tiers of some tools. You pay in time and setup, or in limited use.
  • Monthly subscription: usually tiered by number of songs, length or features.
  • Minute or credit packs: sensible for occasional use, can get expensive for regular practice.
  • One-time licence: common in professional software; major version upgrades may cost extra.

Before deciding, count your own use: how many songs a month do you work on, and how many minutes are they? The right plan is not the same for someone practising three songs a month and someone splitting five a week. Prices change often, so always check the official pricing page on the day you buy. A 20-minute test plan

Trust your ears, not the marketing copy. Test two or three candidates with the same song:

  1. Pick your test song. One you know well and play or sing. Ideally it has a dense chorus, a quiet intro and backing vocals.
  2. Split it in each program and note the time. Include upload, queue and processing.
  3. Solo the vocal stem. Are there traces of cymbals and guitar? Do breaths and "s" sounds come out metallic?
  4. Listen to the instrumental. Can you hear a ghost of the vocal in the chorus? Do drum hits keep their snap?
  5. Solo your own instrument's stem. Is the bass muddy, do guitar and piano blur together?
  6. Do your real job. For practice, slow down and loop the hard section; for production, drop the stems into your DAW. Count the clicks and the number of programs it takes.

The question to ask at the end: "With which one did I practise or deliver this song with the least friction?" The answer is not always the program with the longest feature list.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best stem separation software?

There is no single best for everyone. It depends on whether your song can be uploaded, your computer, how many stems you need, what you do after the split and your budget. Test two or three candidates with the same song.

Is offline stem separation worse than online tools?

Quality depends on the model and the song, not on where the processing happens. The difference with local processing is that your song never leaves your computer and there is no upload or queue. Speed depends on your computer.

How many stems do I need?

Two stems are enough for karaoke or an a cappella. Four stems usually cover drum and bass practice. If you want to hear guitar or piano on their own, choose a program that offers six stems.

Are free stem splitters good enough?

Some separate very well. The cost is setup, model choice and the lack of practice tools. If you only need files they may be enough; if you want to slow down, loop and see chords you will need extra tools.

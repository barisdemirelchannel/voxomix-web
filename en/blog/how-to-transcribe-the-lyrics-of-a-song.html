How to Transcribe the Lyrics of a Song (and Check Every Line)

Isolate the voice, get a timed transcript, then check the unclear lines by ear

Published: 7 November 2026 · ~8 min read

Short answer: to transcribe the lyrics of a song, first separate the vocals from the band, then run a speech-recognition model over that vocal track so each word gets a timestamp. In VoxoMix this happens on your own computer after the song is split: open Song Lyrics and the words scroll along with playback. Then check the lines that sound unclear by looping and slowing them down.

Below: why lyrics are hard to hear in a full mix, what the software actually does, a step-by-step walkthrough, and a simple way to correct the transcript so you can sing or play along with confidence.

Why are lyrics so hard to catch by ear?

Writing down the words of a song by ear usually means pausing, rewinding and guessing. There are a few reasons it is harder than it sounds:

  • The voice is buried in the mix. Guitars, keys and cymbals sit in the same frequency range as consonants, so the "t", "s" and "k" sounds that make words clear get masked.
  • Singers stretch and bend words. A long note can turn one syllable into three, and vowels change shape at high pitch.
  • Backing vocals overlap the lead. Harmonies and doubles land on top of the main line, so two phrases blur into one.
  • Your brain fills the gaps. When a word is unclear, you hear the nearest word you already know. That is how misheard lyrics are born, and once you have sung one wrong for weeks, it is hard to unlearn.

The first fix is the same one a sound engineer would use: get rid of everything that is not the voice. If you want to know more about that step on its own, see our guide on how to remove vocals from a song. The same separation that removes the voice for karaoke can also give you the voice on its own.

How does automatic lyrics transcription work?

An automatic lyrics transcriber does three jobs in a row:

  1. Separation. An AI model splits the song into stems: vocals, drums, bass, guitar, piano and the rest. Transcription works on the vocal stem, not on the full mix, so the band no longer covers the words.
  2. Speech recognition. A multilingual speech model listens to the vocal stem and writes down what it hears. It detects the language of the song by itself, so you do not have to pick one.
  3. Timing. Each word gets a start and end time. That is what lets the lyrics follow the music and lets you jump to a line by clicking it.

In VoxoMix all three steps run on your computer. The song file is not uploaded to a server for separation or transcription. That matters for anyone working on unreleased songs, demos or their own lyrics: the words never leave your machine. If you want the longer explanation of why local processing matters, read offline stem separation.

Good to know: transcription starts in the background as soon as separation finishes, so the lyrics are often ready by the time you open the song. Speech recognition is the heaviest analysis step, so on a slower computer the first pass can take a minute or more for a full-length song.

Step by step: transcribing lyrics in VoxoMix

  1. Add the song. Drag the MP3 (or another audio file) into VoxoMix and start the separation.
  2. Wait for the analysis. After the stems are ready, the app analyses beats, chords, key and lyrics. You can keep working on another song in the meantime.
  3. Open the lyrics. In the player, click Song Lyrics. The Lyrics & Chords window opens with the transcript.
  4. Follow along. Press play. The line being sung lights up, and the words that have already been sung are marked as the song moves on. During long instrumental parts you see a short music placeholder instead of a stuck line.
  5. Jump to a line. Click any line to move playback straight to it. This is the fastest way to replay a phrase you are not sure about.
  6. Re-analyze if needed. If the transcript looks off, use Re-analyze lyrics to run the analysis again.

How do you check and correct the transcript?

No transcriber gets every word right, and a person does not either. Clear pop vocals usually come out well; whispered lines, heavy effects, fast rap, screaming and thick backing vocals are where mistakes appear. Treat the transcript as a strong first draft and check it like this:

  1. Listen through once with the lyrics on screen. Mark the lines where what you read and what you hear disagree.
  2. Solo the vocal stem. Mute drums, bass and guitar and listen to the voice alone. Many "unclear" words become obvious without the band.
  3. Loop the line. Set an A–B loop around the phrase so it repeats on its own. Our guide on looping a section of a song shows how.
  4. Slow it down. Lower the speed with the slider; the pitch stays the same, so the voice does not turn deep and muddy. More on that in slowing down a song without changing the pitch.
  5. Compare with an official source when one exists, such as the artist's release notes or a licensed lyrics service. Use your transcript to find where your ears went wrong, not to argue with the artist.

Write your corrected version in a notebook or text file. Checking the lines yourself is also a good ear-training exercise: after a few songs you start hearing words through a full mix much more easily.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting the first draft completely. Check every line you plan to sing in front of people.
  • Transcribing the full mix. Without separation the band covers the consonants; always work from the vocal stem.
  • Ignoring backing vocals. If two voices sing different words, solo the vocal and listen for which one is the lead.
  • Slowing down with a tool that changes the pitch. A lower, slower voice is harder to understand, not easier.
  • Publishing the lyrics. Song lyrics are protected by copyright. A transcript for your own practice is one thing; posting or printing it for others needs the rights holder's permission.

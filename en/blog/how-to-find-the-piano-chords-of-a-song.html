How to Find the Piano Chords of a Song (and Play It by Ear)

Hear the bass note, see the chord on the keys, slow it down and play along with both hands

Published: 19 October 2026 · ~8 min read

Short answer: to find the piano chords of a song, first listen for the bass note (it is usually the root of the chord), then decide whether the chord is major or minor, and check your answer by playing along with the track. You can do all of this by ear. If you want to move faster, open the song in an app that detects chords automatically and lights them up on an on-screen piano keyboard, slow the song down, and confirm each chord with your ears.

Why finding chords on piano is different from guitar

On guitar, a chord is usually one hand shape: learn the shape and you are done. On piano, the same chord can be voiced dozens of ways. C major can be played as C-E-G, E-G-C or G-C-E. And pianists play with two hands: the left hand usually takes the bass note, the right hand plays the chord itself.

So when you work out a song on piano, you are answering two separate questions:

  1. Which chord is playing? Its name and type: C major, A minor, G7 and so on.
  2. How is it voiced? The order of the notes in your right hand (the inversion) and which note your left hand holds.

The first is enough for a basic accompaniment; the second makes you sound like the record. We cover general chord-finding methods in our guide on how to find the chords of a song; here the focus is entirely on piano.

Start with your ears: bass note, chord colour, check

You can work out the piano chords of a song without any tools. This order works:

1. Find the bass note

Listen only to the lowest sound and search for it with your left hand, one key at a time, until your note "merges" with the bass. In most pop and rock songs the bass plays the root of the chord, so if the bass note is D, the chord is most likely some kind of D chord.

2. Major or minor?

With the root found, play its major and then its minor chord with your right hand. Major tends to sound bright, minor darker. Whichever "sits" with the song is your chord.

3. Check it against the song

Write the chords down in order and play them over the track. If something clashes, either the bass note is not the root (the chord is inverted) or the chord is a richer type, such as a seventh. Listen to that section again.

This trains your ear, but it is slow, especially when piano, guitar and synths overlap in the mix. The method below follows the same logic and adds tools that support your ears.

Reading the on-screen keyboard: Inversion and Basic views

The Chord Finder has two keyboard views:

  • Inversion: a one-octave keyboard. The chord's notes light up within that octave, so you see which notes to play in their simplest form and choose the order yourself. When you want to move between chords with as little hand movement as possible (more on that below), this view makes it easier.
  • Basic: a two-octave keyboard. The chord lights up stacked from its root: root, third, fifth (and the seventh in a seventh chord). If you are learning how a chord is built for the first time, this view is clearer.

If the keyboard feels small, the Enlarge button opens the chord finder in a large window, and the chords keep changing there while the song plays.

The Chord Finder shows major, minor and three seventh types (7, maj7, m7). Colourful chords such as sus, dim, aug or add9 may appear as the nearest basic chord. In those spots, your ear has the final word.

Left hand, right hand: fitting the chord to the song

Naming the chords is half the job. For an accompaniment that sounds like the song, start here:

  • Left hand: the bass note. Play the root of the chord (or the note you hear in the bass channel) low on the keyboard, as a single note or an octave.
  • Right hand: three notes. Play the three notes of the chord in the middle of the keyboard. When you move to the next chord, do not jump: keep common notes where they are and slide the others to the nearest key. Going from C major to A minor, C and E stay put and only G moves up to A. The Inversion view helps you find these close moves.
  • Take the rhythm from the song. On the first pass, play each chord once at the start of the bar. Once that feels solid, copy the rhythm of the piano in the song: solo the keys channel, count the beats and play the same pattern.

When you can play it cleanly at a slow tempo, raise the speed step by step back to the original. If the key does not suit your voice or your hands, use transpose (Basic and higher) to move the song to another key; the chords follow the new key. Where automatic detection can go wrong

Automatic chord detection gives a good starting point for most pop and rock songs, but it is not flawless. Check with your ears in these cases:

  • Inversions and slash chords. If the bass plays a note other than the root (for example C/E), detection may name the chord correctly without telling you the bass note. Listen to the bass channel.
  • Colourful chords. sus, add9 or dim chords may appear as the nearest basic chord.
  • Busy arrangements. When synth pads, strings and guitars share the same range, detection sometimes leans towards the dominant instrument's notes.
  • Fast chord changes. Passing chords that last a single beat can be missed; slow the song down and listen to that section.
  • Length limit. The maximum song length depends on your plan: 4 minutes on the free plan, 5 on Basic, 7 on Student and no limit on Producer.

Conclusion

Finding the piano chords of a song is less daunting than it looks once you work in the right order. Remember three things: find the bass note first, see the chord on the keys and decide major or minor with your ears, and play over the song slowly with the bass in your left hand and the chord in your right. Once the chords sit, bring the speed back up and copy the rhythm of the piano in the song.

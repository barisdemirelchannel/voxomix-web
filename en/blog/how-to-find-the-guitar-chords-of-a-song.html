How to Find the Guitar Chords of a Song: Ear, Diagram and Capo

From the bass note to the chord diagram, from capo maths to playing along, step by step

Published: November 23, 2026 · ~8 min read

Short answer: to find the guitar chords of a song, look for the bass note on your low strings, decide by ear whether the chord is major or minor, then play it along with the track to check it. An automatic chord finder speeds this up by showing the chord name and a fingering while the song plays. Your ear still has the final say.

This method helps when a chord site doesn't have your song, when the version you found sounds wrong, or when you simply want to learn to work songs out yourself. We'll start with nothing but a guitar, then show the same job in VoxoMix. VoxoMix is our own app, so we'll be plain about where it helps and where you still need your ears.

Why is working out chords different on guitar?

On a piano, each note lives in one place. On a guitar, the same chord can be played in several positions with different fingerings. That leaves two separate questions: Which chord is sounding? and Where will I play it? The recording answers the first. The second is your choice.

A capo adds one more twist. If the guitarist on the record used one, the shape they held and the chord you hear are different. Pianists never deal with this, which is why finding piano chords centres on the keyboard, while on guitar shapes and capo maths are part of the job. For the general approach, see our guide to finding the chords of a song.

How do you find chords by ear?

1. Find the bass note

Listen for the lowest note under a chord, then move along the low E or A string one fret at a time until you match it. When the two pitches line up, the clashing feeling disappears. The bass note is often the root, the note that gives the chord its name.

2. Major or minor?

If your root is A, play A major and then A minor over the song. The one that settles into the music is your chord. Major tends to sound brighter and minor darker, but the real test is how it feels against the track. If neither fits, the bass note may not be the root, and automatic detection makes a useful second opinion.

3. Check it against the song

Play your chords in order with the recording. If even one beat rubs the wrong way, mark the spot and go back to that change alone. When a fast passage is hard to follow, slow it down without changing the pitch.

How do you read the chord diagram?

The VoxoMix diagram is horizontal and drawn as if you were facing the guitar. If you're used to vertical chord boxes it can look backwards at first, so four rules are worth knowing:

  • Strings: the top line is the low E string and the bottom line is the high e string.
  • The nut is on the right: frets count from right to left, with fret numbers above the diagram.
  • Markers: a filled dot means press that fret, an empty circle means play the open string, and × means don't play the string.
  • Barre: a bar across several strings means your index finger holds them down together at that fret.

The app shows one shape per chord, so other positions on the neck are yours to explore. For seventh chords the name reads G7 or Am7, while the diagram gives the basic G or Am shape, so you add the seventh yourself for the full colour. As with any chord chart, play each diagram against the song and let your ear confirm it. One caveat: in the version available when this article was published, the diagrams for F and Fm are drawn incorrectly. For those two chords, use these shapes instead: F is 1-3-3-2-1-1 and Fm is 1-3-3-1-1-1, with fret numbers running from the low E string to the high e string.

How do you work out chords with a capo?

Chord detection reports the chord that sounds, not the shape the guitarist held. Someone playing a D shape with a capo at fret 2 is sounding E, so the screen says E. To play with easier shapes, the rule is simple: lower every chord by as many semitones as your capo's fret number. The result is the shape you play.

Here capo 3 is the comfortable choice: instead of barring A#, F, Gm and D#, you play open G, D, Em and C. The app writes notes with sharps, and A# is the same pitch as Bb, just as D# is the same as Eb.

You can also move the song instead of using a capo. Changing the key is available on Basic and above. When you shift the key, the chord name moves to the new key, so follow that name as you play. In the current version the diagram keeps showing the shape for the original key, so pick the shape that matches the new name yourself.

How can you simplify hard chords?

Barre chords are among the shapes that slow practice down the most. Rather than dropping the song, play a simpler substitute first and move to the full shape as your hand adapts. Where can automatic detection go wrong?

No chord finder is perfect on every song. Listen more carefully in these cases:

  • Heavy distortion and dense mixes: overtones and other instruments make the chord harder to read.
  • Chord types: VoxoMix names major, minor, 7, maj7 and m7 chords. Colours such as sus, dim or add9 may appear as the nearest chord.
  • Different bass notes: a chord like C/G may appear as C. Solo the bass channel to hear the difference.
  • Fast changes: very short chords can be missed. Slow the section down and listen again.

How do you fit the chords to the song?

To play along, try this 20-minute plan:

  1. 5 minutes, shapes: with the song stopped, fret each chord in order and check that every string rings clearly.
  2. 5 minutes, the hard change: pick the two chords that trip you up. Turn on Loop and mark the section with A: Start and B: End. Our A–B loop guide has the details.
  3. 5 minutes, slow tempo: set the speed slider to around 0.7. Strum each chord once at the start of the bar, add the rhythm when that feels steady, and raise the speed step by step.
  4. 5 minutes, the real thing: mute the String Instrument channel with M and take the recorded guitarist's place.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest way to find the guitar chords of a song?

Open the song in an app that detects chords, follow the chord name and fingering as it plays, then slow down the hard parts and confirm them by playing along.

Where should I start when working out guitar chords by ear?

Start with the bass note. Find it on the low strings, then try the major and minor chord on that note over the song. A slow song with few chords makes this much easier.

Why do the chords on screen differ from the ones online?

A capo is a common reason. Detection reports the sounding chord, while an online chart may list the capo shapes. If everything is a semitone apart, the recording may also be tuned differently.

Does VoxoMix create guitar tabs?

No. VoxoMix shows the chord name and a chord fingering, but it does not produce tabs for solos or riffs. You can work those out by ear by soloing the guitar channel and slowing it down.

Are guitar chord diagrams included in the free plan?

Does my song leave my computer?

No. Separation and chord analysis run on your Mac (Apple Silicon) or Windows 10/11 computer, and the song is not uploaded. The internet is needed for first sign-in and licence verification, and the lyrics model downloads once the first time you use transcription.

Conclusion

Finding the guitar chords of a song comes down to three questions: what is the bass note, is the chord major or minor, and where will I play it. Automatic detection saves time on the first two, and the diagram gives you a starting point for the third.
